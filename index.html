CHAPTER 01

The New Flavour Map

Flavours are crossing borders faster than brands can follow, and TikTok has become as important as any menu board or restaurant review in deciding what people eat next.

Historically, flavour trends have tended to flow outward from a handful of hubs — LA, New York, London; some of the world’s most culturally diverse melting pots where experimentation thrives and culinary investment has allowed niche ingredients and dishes to gain popularity before hitting mass-market menus and supermarket shelves.

Chapter 01

Social media

Other hubs such as Tokyo, Paris, Lima, Mexico City and Barcelona have also helped export unique flavours and culinary styles across the globe with similar power. But this long-standing pattern of trickle-down food culture is being disrupted; or, at the very least, is now accompanied by an alternative, less hierarchical approach.

TikTok and Instagram work brilliantly as peer-to-peer discovery platforms, enabling regional specialities from smaller markets — sometimes extremely niche — to bypass traditional tastemakers. It’s a boon for diversity and cultural cross-pollination.

Yet, even if TV cooking shows are increasingly rare these days, the traditional culinary hubs — alongside elite dining institutions, critics, and curated rankings — still retain remarkable power, validating trends and giving them lasting commercial weight.

Beyond the algorithm

Now we also have generative AI to consider. At first glance, this looks to be an interesting development, and chatbots could help both develop and spread new trends and ideas to enormous audiences. However, algorithmic dependence also risks creating a homogenising feedback loop if systems draw from the same sources. Indeed, if a genuine trend exists without a digital footprint, AI will overlook it entirely – and this is its weakness. Food culture, after all, comes from local producers and kitchens, and thrives on real-world human subjectivity and tablecloth messiness.

Hyper-regional flavours redraw the menu

Hyper-regional flavours redraw the menu

The shift towards flavour diversity is perhaps best illustrated by the erosion of generic labels — ‘Asian’ and ‘Latin American’ in particular. In their place is an effort to spotlight true regionality. Indeed, Lumina Intelligence’s menu tracker now lists ‘hyper-regional’ as an independent category alongside specific tags like West African and Ethiopian. Businesses are now leveraging this precision to carve out niches, future-proof menus, and create (increasingly desirable) customisable options.

Matcha graduates from café to supermarket, but what comes next?

Matcha graduates from café to supermarket, but what comes next?

Marketed as a source of ‘calm energy’, matcha has transitioned from a niche order to a viridescent high-street powerhouse, driven largely by a youth market and a viral ‘MatchaTok’ trend. Menu listings for the shade-grown tea powder grew over 30% year on year, while café chain Blank Street was estimated to have generated over £2.6m from matcha sales in June and July 2025 alone.

We’re in a real pickle here

We’re in a real pickle here

Oh, how we love the ancient wonder that is pickle. And in 2026 we’re witnessing something of a mouth-watering surge in popularity. Spanish crisp-maker Torres, known for upmarket flavours like black truffle, has added pickle to its range; Germany's Katja has launched pickle-flavoured sweets; and McDonald's introduced gherkin-seasoned fries. News of KFC's own pickle menu, including a Pepsi Max pickle juice drink, ranked as the single most-read flavour-led story on The Grocer in 2026. Pickle-infused cocktails and martinis are also edging into European bars.

Texture overtakes flavour in product development

Texture overtakes flavour in product development

Out of new ingredients to try? Food developers increasingly rank texture on par with flavour, suggesting there is a different way to explore new product ideas. Certainly, industry R&D directors have made it clear that mouthfeel is now a primary innovation driver, pointing to the multi-textural contrasts behind viral hits like Dubai chocolate. Crucially, this phenomenon arose on social media long before industrial manufacturers launched packaged versions. The arc of Dubai chocolate therefore illustrates a standard 12-to-18-month lag between online consumer trends and mass-market supermarket distribution.

Tinned fish becomes a lifestyle category

Tinned fish becomes a lifestyle category

Mussels, mackerel and sardines are now being marketed on protein content, sustainability and affordability all at once; but there is a growing premiumisation angle too, meaning tinned fish is leveraging multiple narratives simultaneously. Heritage European canneries are gaining renewed attention alongside newer entrants, as tinned fish shifts from a store-cupboard staple into something closer to a lifestyle purchase.

The Bear effect

The Bear effect

Beloved TV series The Bear exposed the razor-thin margins, burnout and closures threatening independent hospitality. The series gave viewers a stark look at the industry's harsh economic realities. It wasn’t just about feeling inspired about food; viewers were gaining a deeper understanding and respect for the graft of producing it. As the show reached its final season in 2026, it stands as a watershed moment for food and drink culture.
